1 Why hypertension matters

High blood pressure (hypertension) is one of the most common chronic conditions in the United States and one of the most important. It usually causes no symptoms, yet it is a major risk factor for heart disease and stroke, the leading causes of death in the country, as well as for kidney disease. Because it is silent, many people do not know they have it until damage has already occurred.

It is also common. Using the 2017 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) definition, the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) estimated that 45.4% of U.S. adults had hypertension in 2017–2018 [1] and 47.7% in August 2021–August 2023 [2]. Both reports found that prevalence rises sharply with age and is higher in men than in women. That combination of a high burden, a long symptom-free period, and well-established treatments makes it worth understanding who is most likely to have high blood pressure.

For this project I asked a focused version of that question:

What demographic and anthropometric factors are associated with hypertension among U.S. adults?

2 Why NHANES is a good fit

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) is run by the CDC’s National Center for Health Statistics. It is well suited to this question for three reasons:

  1. Blood pressure and body size are measured, not self-reported. Each participant visits a Mobile Examination Center, where trained staff take up to three oscillometric blood pressure readings and measure height and weight. Self-reported weight tends to be underestimated, and many people with high blood pressure do not know it, so measured values are a real advantage.
  2. It links examination and interview data for the same person through a shared identifier (SEQN). That let me combine measured blood pressure with a questionnaire item about blood-pressure medication, which is needed to identify people whose hypertension is controlled by treatment.
  3. It is large and diverse. NHANES deliberately over-samples several groups, including Hispanic, Non-Hispanic Black, and Non-Hispanic Asian adults, so there are enough people in every race/ethnicity group to compare them.

I used the 2017–March 2020 pre-pandemic release, as required for Project A. It combines the full 2017–2018 cycle with the part of the 2019–2020 cycle completed before fieldwork stopped in March 2020 because of COVID-19.

3 Research question and hypotheses

Before looking at any results, I wrote down four hypotheses based on the NCHS reports and on basic physiology:

Hypothesis Main reasoning
H1 Older adults have a higher prevalence of hypertension. Arteries stiffen with age, raising systolic pressure.
H2 Individuals with higher BMI have a higher prevalence of hypertension. Excess body weight is linked to higher blood volume, sympathetic activity, and insulin resistance.
H3 Hypertension prevalence differs by biological sex. National reports show higher prevalence in men, especially at younger ages.
H4 Hypertension prevalence differs across race/ethnicity groups. National reports show the highest prevalence among Non-Hispanic Black adults.

4 Data and variables

I merged four NHANES tables by SEQN:

  • P_DEMO (Demographics): interview/examination status, age, sex, and race/ethnicity.
  • P_BMX (Body Measures): measured body mass index (BMI).
  • P_BPXO (Blood Pressure – Oscillometric): up to three systolic and three diastolic readings.
  • P_BPQ (Blood Pressure & Cholesterol questionnaire): whether the participant currently takes prescribed medicine for high blood pressure.

This meets the Project A requirement of using the Demographics file plus at least two files from the Examination, Laboratory, or Questionnaire groups (here, two Examination files and one Questionnaire file). The codebook below lists every variable in the final analytic data set.

4.1 How I defined hypertension

My primary outcome follows the definition used in both NCHS reports [1, 2], which comes from the 2017 ACC/AHA guideline [3]: a participant has hypertension if their average systolic pressure is at least 130 mmHg, or their average diastolic pressure is at least 80 mmHg, or they report currently taking prescribed medicine for high blood pressure. Including medication matters: a person whose blood pressure is well controlled by treatment still has hypertension.

I was careful not to over-read what this variable measures. NHANES measures blood pressure at a single visit, while a clinical diagnosis requires elevated readings on at least two separate occasions. My outcome is therefore a survey classification of hypertension, which is what national surveillance reports use, rather than a clinical diagnosis. Because the 130/80 threshold is newer and lower than the older 140/90 threshold from the JNC 7 report, I also repeated the main analyses with the 140/90 definition to see whether any conclusion depended on that choice.

5 Data preprocessing

All downloading, merging, and cleaning happens in one R script (R/prepare_nhanes_data.R, shown in full in the appendix), so the process can be rerun from the raw public files. The steps were:

  1. Download the four tables with the nhanesA package, keeping the original numeric codes (translated = FALSE) so that every recode is explicit, and cache each raw table as an .Rds file so later renders do not download it again.
  2. Merge the tables with left_join() on SEQN, starting from Demographics, and standardize names with janitor::clean_names().
  3. Restrict to participants who were both interviewed and examined (RIDSTATR = 2) and aged 21 to 79. NHANES records everyone aged 80 and over as “80,” so their true age is unknown; the course guidance also recommends analyzing adults only.
  4. Average blood pressure over the available oscillometric readings, treating any diastolic reading of 0 mmHg as missing.
  5. Code medication use while respecting the questionnaire’s skip pattern. Only people who had been told they had high blood pressure (BPQ020) and had been told to take medicine for it (BPQ040A) were asked whether they currently take it (BPQ050A). Anyone who answered “no” to an earlier question was coded as not taking medication, and “Refused” or “Don’t know” answers were set to missing.
  6. Label factors with meaningful levels and sensible reference groups (Female for sex; Non-Hispanic White, the largest group, for race/ethnicity).
  7. Keep complete cases on every analytic variable, then run automatic checks (stopifnot()) that the sample size falls within the course range of 500 to 7,500, that identifiers are unique, that ages, BMI, and blood pressure are within plausible ranges, and that every race/ethnicity group has at least 100 participants.
  8. Create display categories for age and BMI. Age and BMI stay continuous in the regression model; the categories are used only to show prevalence in tables and bar charts.

6 Statistical methods

Following the course convention, I report 90% CIs and use a significance level of α = 0.10 throughout. I chose each method to match the type of comparison:

  • Prevalence within groups: proportion with hypertension, with Wilson score confidence intervals, which behave well even for smaller groups.
  • Association between a grouping variable and hypertension: Pearson chi-square test of independence. For the two ordered variables (age group and BMI category), I added the Cochran–Armitage test for trend, which asks the more specific question of whether prevalence rises (or falls) steadily across ordered groups.
  • Sex (a 2 × 2 table): difference in prevalence between men and women with a 90% CI.
  • BMI by hypertension status: Welch two-sample t-test for the difference in mean BMI. BMI is right-skewed, but with thousands of participants per group the sampling distribution of the mean is close to normal; I also report medians as a check.
  • Multivariable model: logistic regression of hypertension on age (per 10 years), BMI (per 5 kg/m²), sex, and race/ethnicity. I present adjusted odds ratios with profile-likelihood 90% CIs next to unadjusted odds ratios, a likelihood-ratio test for each variable (which gives one overall test for the six-level race/ethnicity variable), the C-statistic for discrimination, and a decile calibration plot.
  • Sensitivity analysis: the same model refit with the 140/90 mmHg definition.

Rescaling age to 10-year units and BMI to 5-unit steps does not change the model; it only makes each odds ratio describe a difference that is meaningful in practice.

7 Building the dashboard

I approached the dashboard as one pass through the PPDAC cycle (Problem, Plan, Data, Analysis, Conclusion). The problem was the research question above; the plan was the four hypotheses and the list of figures that would answer each one; the data step became the preprocessing script; the analysis became a second shared script; and the conclusion is the final dashboard page and this post.

I built it with Quarto’s dashboard format inside my existing Quarto website, so it shares the site’s navigation bar, colors, and fonts, and it renders and deploys with the rest of the site. The code is split into three layers:

  • R/prepare_nhanes_data.R downloads, merges, cleans, and checks the data;
  • R/htn_analysis.R runs every test and model and defines every figure;
  • the dashboard and this post only display those results.

This separation means the dashboard and the post cannot disagree, and a change to the analysis only has to be made once.

9 Interpretation

Taken together, the results describe a clear pattern in this sample. The age gradient was large (from 26% to 78% across the three age groups), which fits the well-known stiffening of large arteries with age and the NCHS finding that more than 70% of adults aged 60 and over have hypertension [2]. The BMI odds ratio was estimated after accounting for age, sex, and race/ethnicity, so the BMI pattern is not simply a by-product of differences in age or the other variables. Men had higher adjusted odds than women, the same direction as in the national reports [1, 2].

The race/ethnicity results need the most careful reading. Race/ethnicity is a self-reported social category, not a biological measurement, so differences between groups are best understood as markers of differences in social and economic conditions, access to care, stress, diet, and other exposures that this analysis did not measure. The adjusted model tells us whether a gap remains after accounting for age, BMI, and sex; it does not tell us why it exists.

Finally, it is important to be precise about what “hypertension” means here. It is a classification based on blood pressure measured at one visit plus self-reported medication, which is how national surveillance defines it, but it is not the same as a clinical diagnosis.

10 Limitations

  • Unweighted analysis. I did not use the NHANES sampling weights, strata, or clusters, as specified for Project A. Because NHANES over-samples some groups, my percentages describe this sample and are not estimates of national prevalence.
  • Cross-sectional data. The survey measures everything at one time, so it can show association but not cause and effect, or which came first.
  • Single-visit blood pressure. Readings from one visit can be affected by temporary factors such as the “white coat” effect, which may lead to some misclassification.
  • Self-reported medication. Medication use depends on accurate recall and on the questionnaire’s skip pattern.
  • Omitted variables. Income, education, smoking, alcohol, physical activity, diet, diabetes, and kidney function were not included and may confound the associations shown.
  • Model assumptions. The model assumes that age and BMI have a straight-line relationship with the log-odds of hypertension, and BMI is only an indirect measure of body fat. Odds ratios also overstate risk ratios when an outcome is this common.
  • Complete-case analysis. Excluding participants with missing data could bias the results if missingness was related to blood pressure.
  • Data age. The data were collected before March 2020; patterns may have changed since then.

11 Conclusions

In 7,019 NHANES adults aged 21–79, hypertension was common (54.6% of the sample under the 130/80 definition). After adjustment for the other factors, each additional 10 years of age carried 1.94 times the odds of hypertension, each additional 5 kg/m² of BMI carried 1.39 times the odds, and men had 1.51 times the odds of women; the overall test for race/ethnicity gave p < 0.001. The hypothesis scorecard in Table 5 summarizes the formal decision for each hypothesis.

Because PPDAC is a cycle, these conclusions point to the next problem to study. A natural second cycle would (1) use the survey weights to produce national estimates, (2) add socioeconomic and lifestyle variables to understand why the race/ethnicity and sex differences exist, (3) allow non-linear age and BMI effects, (4) extend the analysis to the August 2021–August 2023 NHANES cycle, and (5) study hypertension awareness and control, which are more directly actionable for public health than prevalence alone.

# Read one NHANES table, using a cached .Rds copy when it exists.
# translated = FALSE keeps the original numeric codes (e.g., RIAGENDR = 1/2)
# so that every recode below is explicit and documented here.
fetch_nhanes_table <- function(table_name, raw_dir) {
  cache_file <- file.path(raw_dir, paste0(table_name, ".Rds"))
  if (file.exists(cache_file)) {
    return(readRDS(cache_file))
  }

  raw_table <- tryCatch(
    nhanesA::nhanes(table_name, translated = FALSE),
    error = function(e) NULL
  )

  # Fallback: read the SAS transport file directly from the CDC website
  # (used only if nhanesA cannot reach the CDC server).
  if (is.null(raw_table) || nrow(raw_table) == 0) {
    cdc_urls <- c(
      sprintf("https://wwwn.cdc.gov/Nchs/Data/Nhanes/Public/2017/DataFiles/%s.xpt",
              table_name),
      sprintf("https://wwwn.cdc.gov/Nchs/Nhanes/2017-2018/%s.XPT", table_name)
    )
    for (url in cdc_urls) {
      xpt_file <- tempfile(fileext = ".xpt")
      ok <- tryCatch({
        utils::download.file(url, xpt_file, mode = "wb", quiet = TRUE)
        TRUE
      }, error = function(e) FALSE, warning = function(w) FALSE)
      if (ok) {
        raw_table <- haven::zap_labels(haven::read_xpt(xpt_file))
        break
      }
    }
  }

  if (is.null(raw_table) || nrow(raw_table) == 0) {
    stop("Could not download NHANES table ", table_name,
         ". Check your internet connection and try again.", call. = FALSE)
  }

  raw_table <- as_tibble(raw_table)
  saveRDS(raw_table, cache_file)
  raw_table
}

# Merge the four tables and build the analytic tibble.
build_htn_data <- function(raw_dir) {

  demo_raw <- fetch_nhanes_table("P_DEMO", raw_dir)
  bmx_raw  <- fetch_nhanes_table("P_BMX",  raw_dir)
  bpxo_raw <- fetch_nhanes_table("P_BPXO", raw_dir)
  bpq_raw  <- fetch_nhanes_table("P_BPQ",  raw_dir)

  # Merge on the respondent identifier SEQN (Demographics is the base table),
  # then standardise names with janitor::clean_names().
  merged <- demo_raw |>
    select(SEQN, RIDSTATR, RIDAGEYR, RIAGENDR, RIDRETH3) |>
    left_join(select(bmx_raw, SEQN, BMXBMI), by = "SEQN") |>
    left_join(select(bpxo_raw, SEQN, BPXOSY1, BPXODI1, BPXOSY2, BPXODI2,
                     BPXOSY3, BPXODI3), by = "SEQN") |>
    left_join(select(bpq_raw, SEQN, BPQ020, BPQ040A, BPQ050A), by = "SEQN") |>
    clean_names()

  n_all <- nrow(merged)

  # Eligible population: interviewed AND examined (RIDSTATR = 2), ages 21-79.
  eligible <- merged |>
    filter(ridstatr == 2, ridageyr >= age_min, ridageyr <= age_max)

  n_eligible <- nrow(eligible)

  cleaned <- eligible |>
    mutate(
      # Blood pressure: mean of the available oscillometric readings
      # (NCHS convention). A diastolic value of 0 is not physiologic, so it is
      # treated as missing.
      across(c(bpxodi1, bpxodi2, bpxodi3), ~ na_if(.x, 0)),
      sbp_mean = rowMeans(pick(bpxosy1, bpxosy2, bpxosy3), na.rm = TRUE),
      dbp_mean = rowMeans(pick(bpxodi1, bpxodi2, bpxodi3), na.rm = TRUE),
      sbp_mean = if_else(is.nan(sbp_mean), NA_real_, sbp_mean),
      dbp_mean = if_else(is.nan(dbp_mean), NA_real_, dbp_mean),

      # Current antihypertensive medication, respecting the questionnaire's
      # skip pattern: people never told they had high BP (BPQ020 = 2), or
      # never told to take medicine (BPQ040A = 2), were not asked BPQ050A and
      # are coded as not taking medication. Refused (7) / Don't know (9)
      # become missing.
      bp_med = case_when(
        bpq050a == 1 ~ 1L,
        bpq050a == 2 ~ 0L,
        bpq040a == 2 ~ 0L,
        bpq020  == 2 ~ 0L,
        .default = NA_integer_
      ),

      # Demographics
      age = ridageyr,
      sex = factor(riagendr, levels = c(2, 1), labels = c("Female", "Male")),
      race_eth = factor(
        ridreth3,
        levels = c(3, 4, 1, 2, 6, 7),
        labels = c("Non-Hispanic White", "Non-Hispanic Black",
                   "Mexican American", "Other Hispanic",
                   "Non-Hispanic Asian", "Other/Multiracial")
      ),

      # Anthropometry
      bmi = bmxbmi
    ) |>
    # Complete cases on every analytic variable (course requirement)
    filter(!is.na(age), !is.na(sex), !is.na(race_eth), !is.na(bmi),
           !is.na(sbp_mean), !is.na(dbp_mean), !is.na(bp_med))

  n_complete <- nrow(cleaned)

  # Keep within the course's 7,500-subject ceiling (reproducible sample).
  if (n_complete > max_sample_size) {
    set.seed(431)
    cleaned <- slice_sample(cleaned, n = max_sample_size)
  }

  htn_data <- cleaned |>
    mutate(
      # PRIMARY outcome - 2017 ACC/AHA definition used by NCHS
      # (NCHS Data Brief No. 364): mean SBP >= 130 or mean DBP >= 80 mmHg,
      # or currently taking antihypertensive medication.
      htn = as.integer(sbp_mean >= 130 | dbp_mean >= 80 | bp_med == 1),

      # SENSITIVITY outcome - JNC 7 definition: >= 140 / >= 90 mmHg or
      # medication. Used only to check that conclusions do not depend on the
      # threshold chosen.
      htn_jnc7 = as.integer(sbp_mean >= 140 | dbp_mean >= 90 | bp_med == 1),

      htn_label = factor(htn, levels = c(0, 1),
                         labels = c("No hypertension", "Hypertension")),

      # Display groupings. Age and BMI stay CONTINUOUS in the regression model;
      # these categories are used only for prevalence tables and figures.
      # Age groups follow NCHS reporting bands; BMI groups follow CDC/WHO
      # adult cut-points (obesity classes 2 and 3 are combined for group size).
      age_group = cut(age, breaks = c(20, 39, 59, 79),
                      labels = c("21-39", "40-59", "60-79")),
      bmi_cat = cut(bmi, breaks = c(-Inf, 18.5, 25, 30, 35, Inf), right = FALSE,
                    labels = c("Underweight (<18.5)",
                               "Healthy weight (18.5-24.9)",
                               "Overweight (25-29.9)",
                               "Obesity class 1 (30-34.9)",
                               "Obesity class 2-3 (35+)")),

      # Rescaled predictors so odds ratios describe meaningful contrasts
      age_10 = age / 10,   # per 10 years of age
      bmi_5  = bmi / 5     # per 5 kg/m^2 of BMI
    ) |>
    droplevels() |>
    select(seqn, age, age_group, age_10, sex, race_eth, bmi, bmi_cat, bmi_5,
           sbp_mean, dbp_mean, bp_med, htn, htn_label, htn_jnc7) |>
    arrange(seqn)

  # ---- Sanity checks (stop the render rather than show wrong numbers) ------
  stopifnot(
    "Every subject must be interviewed and examined" =
      all(eligible$ridstatr == 2),
    "SEQN must be unique" = !anyDuplicated(htn_data$seqn),
    "Sample size must satisfy the 500-7,500 course rule" =
      between(nrow(htn_data), min_sample_size, max_sample_size),
    "Age must lie in 21-79" = all(between(htn_data$age, age_min, age_max)),
    "BMI must be physiologically plausible" =
      all(between(htn_data$bmi, 10, 100)),
    "Mean SBP must be physiologically plausible" =
      all(between(htn_data$sbp_mean, 60, 260)),
    "No missing values in the analytic tibble" =
      !anyNA(htn_data),
    "Each race/ethnicity group needs at least 100 subjects" =
      all(table(htn_data$race_eth) >= 100)
  )

  provenance <- list(
    cycle        = "NHANES 2017-March 2020 pre-pandemic",
    tables       = nhanes_tables,
    downloaded   = as.Date(min(file.info(
      file.path(raw_dir, paste0(nhanes_tables, ".Rds")))$mtime)),
    built        = Sys.Date(),
    n_all        = n_all,
    n_eligible   = n_eligible,
    n_complete   = n_complete,
    n_analytic   = nrow(htn_data),
    nhanesA_version = as.character(utils::packageVersion("nhanesA"))
  )

  list(data = htn_data, provenance = provenance)
}

# Returns the analytic tibble; the provenance list is attached as an attribute.
load_htn_data <- function(site_root, rebuild = FALSE) {
  data_dir <- file.path(site_root, "supporting-data")
  raw_dir  <- file.path(data_dir, "raw")
  dir.create(raw_dir, recursive = TRUE, showWarnings = FALSE)

  analytic_file   <- file.path(data_dir, "nhanes_htn_analytic.rds")
  provenance_file <- file.path(data_dir, "data_provenance.rds")

  if (rebuild || !file.exists(analytic_file) || !file.exists(provenance_file)) {
    built <- build_htn_data(raw_dir)
    saveRDS(built$data, analytic_file)
    saveRDS(built$provenance, provenance_file)
    write_csv(built$data, file.path(data_dir, "nhanes_htn_analytic.csv"))
  }

  htn_data <- readRDS(analytic_file)
  attr(htn_data, "provenance") <- readRDS(provenance_file)
  htn_data
}

# Wilson score interval for a proportion (vectorised; good coverage even
# when a group is small or the proportion is extreme).
wilson_ci <- function(cases, n, conf = ci_level) {
  z      <- qnorm(1 - (1 - conf) / 2)
  p_hat  <- cases / n
  denom  <- 1 + z^2 / n
  center <- (p_hat + z^2 / (2 * n)) / denom
  half   <- z * sqrt(p_hat * (1 - p_hat) / n + z^2 / (4 * n^2)) / denom
  tibble(lower = center - half, upper = center + half)
}

# Hypertension prevalence (with Wilson CI) within levels of one grouping
# variable. `outcome` defaults to the primary definition.
summarise_prevalence <- function(data, group_var, outcome = htn) {
  data |>
    group_by({{ group_var }}) |>
    summarise(n = n(), cases = sum({{ outcome }}), .groups = "drop") |>
    mutate(prevalence = cases / n,
           wilson_ci(cases, n),
           pct_of_sample = n / sum(n))
}

# Chi-square test of independence (group x hypertension). For ordered groups
# the Cochran-Armitage test for trend is also returned.
test_prevalence <- function(data, group_var, ordered = FALSE) {
  group_values <- pull(data, {{ group_var }})
  chi <- suppressWarnings(chisq.test(table(group_values, data$htn)))
  out <- tibble(
    test      = "Pearson chi-square test of independence",
    statistic = unname(chi$statistic),
    df        = unname(chi$parameter),
    p_value   = chi$p.value
  )
  if (ordered) {
    counts <- summarise_prevalence(data, {{ group_var }})
    trend  <- suppressWarnings(prop.trend.test(counts$cases, counts$n))
    out <- bind_rows(out, tibble(
      test      = "Cochran-Armitage test for trend",
      statistic = unname(trend$statistic),
      df        = unname(trend$parameter),
      p_value   = trend$p.value
    ))
  }
  out
}

# Harrell's C-statistic (area under the ROC curve) via the rank formula.
c_statistic <- function(observed, predicted) {
  ranks <- rank(predicted)
  n_1 <- sum(observed == 1)
  n_0 <- sum(observed == 0)
  (sum(ranks[observed == 1]) - n_1 * (n_1 + 1) / 2) / (n_1 * n_0)
}

# ---- Main analysis ---------------------------------------------------------------

run_htn_analysis <- function(htn_data) {

  results <- list()

  # -- Sample summary (KPI cards) ------------------------------------------------
  htn_ci <- wilson_ci(sum(htn_data$htn), nrow(htn_data))
  results$kpi <- tibble(
    n            = nrow(htn_data),
    mean_age     = mean(htn_data$age),
    sd_age       = sd(htn_data$age),
    mean_bmi     = mean(htn_data$bmi),
    sd_bmi       = sd(htn_data$bmi),
    prevalence   = mean(htn_data$htn),
    prev_lower   = htn_ci$lower,
    prev_upper   = htn_ci$upper,
    prevalence_jnc7 = mean(htn_data$htn_jnc7),
    pct_on_meds  = mean(htn_data$bp_med)
  )

  # -- Prevalence by group, with tests ---------------------------------------------
  results$prev_age  <- summarise_prevalence(htn_data, age_group)
  results$prev_bmi  <- summarise_prevalence(htn_data, bmi_cat)
  results$prev_sex  <- summarise_prevalence(htn_data, sex)
  results$prev_race <- summarise_prevalence(htn_data, race_eth)

  results$test_age  <- test_prevalence(htn_data, age_group, ordered = TRUE)
  results$test_bmi  <- test_prevalence(htn_data, bmi_cat,   ordered = TRUE)
  results$test_sex  <- test_prevalence(htn_data, sex)
  results$test_race <- test_prevalence(htn_data, race_eth)

  # Sensitivity: prevalence by group using the >= 140/90 definition
  results$prev_jnc7 <- bind_rows(
    summarise_prevalence(htn_data, age_group, htn_jnc7) |>
      mutate(factor = "Age group", level = as.character(age_group)),
    summarise_prevalence(htn_data, bmi_cat, htn_jnc7) |>
      mutate(factor = "BMI category", level = as.character(bmi_cat)),
    summarise_prevalence(htn_data, sex, htn_jnc7) |>
      mutate(factor = "Sex", level = as.character(sex)),
    summarise_prevalence(htn_data, race_eth, htn_jnc7) |>
      mutate(factor = "Race/ethnicity", level = as.character(race_eth))
  ) |>
    select(factor, level, n, cases, prevalence, lower, upper)

  # -- Sex: 2 x 2 comparison (risk difference and its CI) ---------------------------
  sex_counts <- results$prev_sex
  sex_prop   <- prop.test(x = rev(sex_counts$cases), n = rev(sex_counts$n),
                          conf.level = ci_level, correct = FALSE)
  results$sex_risk_diff <- tibble(
    comparison = "Male minus female",
    difference = unname(sex_prop$estimate[1] - sex_prop$estimate[2]),
    lower      = sex_prop$conf.int[1],
    upper      = sex_prop$conf.int[2],
    p_value    = sex_prop$p.value
  )

  # -- BMI (continuous) by hypertension status: Welch two-sample t-test --------------
  bmi_t <- t.test(bmi ~ htn_label, data = htn_data, conf.level = ci_level)
  results$bmi_by_htn <- htn_data |>
    group_by(htn_label) |>
    summarise(n = n(), mean_bmi = mean(bmi), sd_bmi = sd(bmi),
              median_bmi = median(bmi), .groups = "drop")
  results$bmi_ttest <- tibble(
    # t.test orders groups as "No hypertension" then "Hypertension";
    # reverse so the difference is Hypertension minus No hypertension.
    difference = unname(bmi_t$estimate[2] - bmi_t$estimate[1]),
    lower      = -bmi_t$conf.int[2],
    upper      = -bmi_t$conf.int[1],
    statistic  = unname(bmi_t$statistic),
    df         = unname(bmi_t$parameter),
    p_value    = bmi_t$p.value
  )

  # -- Logistic regression ------------------------------------------------------------
  model_formula <- htn ~ age_10 + bmi_5 + sex + race_eth
  model_primary <- glm(model_formula, family = binomial, data = htn_data)
  model_jnc7    <- glm(update(model_formula, htn_jnc7 ~ .),
                       family = binomial, data = htn_data)

  tidy_or <- function(model) {
    tidy(model, exponentiate = TRUE, conf.int = TRUE, conf.level = ci_level) |>
      filter(term != "(Intercept)") |>
      mutate(label = label_term(term))
  }

  or_adjusted <- tidy_or(model_primary)
  or_jnc7     <- tidy_or(model_jnc7)

  # Unadjusted (one predictor at a time) odds ratios, for comparison
  or_unadjusted <- map(c("age_10", "bmi_5", "sex", "race_eth"), \(predictor) {
    glm(reformulate(predictor, response = "htn"),
        family = binomial, data = htn_data) |>
      tidy_or()
  }) |>
    list_rbind()

  results$or_table <- or_adjusted |>
    select(term, label, adj_or = estimate, adj_low = conf.low,
           adj_high = conf.high, adj_p = p.value) |>
    left_join(or_unadjusted |>
                select(term, crude_or = estimate, crude_low = conf.low,
                       crude_high = conf.high),
              by = "term") |>
    mutate(significant = adj_low > 1 | adj_high < 1)

  results$or_compare <- bind_rows(
    or_adjusted |> mutate(model = "Primary (>= 130/80)"),
    or_jnc7     |> mutate(model = "Sensitivity (>= 140/90)")
  ) |>
    mutate(label = fct_rev(fct_inorder(label)),
           model = factor(model, levels = names(pal_model)))

  # Robustness check: does every odds ratio point in the same direction, and
  # keep the same significance decision, under the 140/90 definition?
  sens_check <- inner_join(
    select(or_adjusted, term, est_primary = estimate, lo_p = conf.low,
           hi_p = conf.high),
    select(or_jnc7, term, est_jnc7 = estimate, lo_j = conf.low,
           hi_j = conf.high),
    by = "term"
  ) |>
    mutate(same_direction = sign(log(est_primary)) == sign(log(est_jnc7)),
           same_decision  = (lo_p > 1 | hi_p < 1) == (lo_j > 1 | hi_j < 1))
  results$sensitivity <- list(
    all_same_direction = all(sens_check$same_direction),
    all_same_decision  = all(sens_check$same_decision),
    changed_terms      = label_term(sens_check$term[!sens_check$same_decision |
                                                  !sens_check$same_direction])
  )

  # Likelihood-ratio test for each term (one p-value for the 6-level
  # race/ethnicity variable)
  results$lrt <- drop1(model_primary, test = "LRT") |>
    as_tibble(rownames = "term") |>
    filter(term != "<none>") |>
    transmute(
      term,
      label = recode(term, age_10 = "Age (per 10 years)",
                     bmi_5 = "BMI (per 5 kg/m²)", sex = "Sex",
                     race_eth = "Race/ethnicity"),
      df = Df, lr_chisq = LRT, p_value = `Pr(>Chi)`
    )

  # Model performance: discrimination and calibration
  fitted_prob <- fitted(model_primary)
  results$performance <- tibble(
    n            = nobs(model_primary),
    c_statistic  = c_statistic(htn_data$htn, fitted_prob),
    aic          = AIC(model_primary),
    mcfadden_r2  = 1 - model_primary$deviance / model_primary$null.deviance,
    c_stat_jnc7  = c_statistic(htn_data$htn_jnc7, fitted(model_jnc7))
  )
  results$calibration <- tibble(observed = htn_data$htn,
                                predicted = fitted_prob) |>
    mutate(decile = ntile(predicted, 10)) |>
    group_by(decile) |>
    summarise(n = n(), mean_predicted = mean(predicted),
              observed = mean(observed), .groups = "drop") |>
    mutate(wilson_ci(observed * n, n))
  results$calibration_max_gap <- max(abs(results$calibration$observed -
                                         results$calibration$mean_predicted))

  # Predicted probability curves: age x BMI, by sex, for the reference
  # race/ethnicity group (Non-Hispanic White). Confidence bands are built on
  # the log-odds scale and back-transformed.
  z_ci <- qnorm(1 - alpha_lvl / 2)
  prediction_grid <- expand_grid(
    age = seq(21, 79, by = 1),
    bmi = c(22.5, 27.5, 32.5, 37.5),
    sex = factor(c("Female", "Male"), levels = levels(htn_data$sex))
  ) |>
    mutate(age_10 = age / 10, bmi_5 = bmi / 5,
           race_eth = factor("Non-Hispanic White",
                             levels = levels(htn_data$race_eth)))
  link_pred <- predict(model_primary, newdata = prediction_grid,
                       type = "link", se.fit = TRUE)
  results$predictions <- prediction_grid |>
    mutate(
      prob  = plogis(link_pred$fit),
      lower = plogis(link_pred$fit - z_ci * link_pred$se.fit),
      upper = plogis(link_pred$fit + z_ci * link_pred$se.fit),
      bmi_label = factor(paste0("BMI ", bmi, " kg/m²"),
                         levels = paste0("BMI ", c(22.5, 27.5, 32.5, 37.5),
                                         " kg/m²"))
    )

  results$model_primary <- model_primary
  results$model_jnc7    <- model_jnc7

  # -- Hypothesis verdicts (decision rule: alpha = 0.10 / 90% CI) ----------------------
  or_row <- function(t) filter(results$or_table, term == t)
  race_p <- filter(results$lrt, term == "race_eth")$p_value
  sex_or <- or_row("sexMale")
  higher_sex <- if (sex_or$adj_or > 1) "men" else "women"

  results$hypotheses <- tibble(
    hypothesis = c("H1: Older adults have a higher prevalence of hypertension.",
                   "H2: Higher BMI is associated with a higher prevalence of hypertension.",
                   "H3: Hypertension prevalence differs by biological sex.",
                   "H4: Hypertension prevalence differs across race/ethnicity groups."),
    evidence = c(
      paste0("Trend test p ", fmt_p_inline(results$test_age$p_value[2]),
             "; adjusted OR per 10 years ",
             fmt_or(or_row("age_10")$adj_or, or_row("age_10")$adj_low,
                    or_row("age_10")$adj_high)),
      paste0("Trend test p ", fmt_p_inline(results$test_bmi$p_value[2]),
             "; adjusted OR per 5 kg/m² ",
             fmt_or(or_row("bmi_5")$adj_or, or_row("bmi_5")$adj_low,
                    or_row("bmi_5")$adj_high)),
      paste0("Chi-square p ", fmt_p_inline(results$test_sex$p_value[1]),
             "; adjusted OR (male vs. female) ",
             fmt_or(sex_or$adj_or, sex_or$adj_low, sex_or$adj_high)),
      paste0("Chi-square p ", fmt_p_inline(results$test_race$p_value[1]),
             "; adjusted likelihood-ratio test p ", fmt_p_inline(race_p))
    ),
    supported = c(
      results$test_age$p_value[2] < alpha_lvl & or_row("age_10")$adj_low > 1,
      results$test_bmi$p_value[2] < alpha_lvl & or_row("bmi_5")$adj_low > 1,
      results$test_sex$p_value[1] < alpha_lvl & sex_or$significant,
      results$test_race$p_value[1] < alpha_lvl & race_p < alpha_lvl
    )
  ) |>
    mutate(verdict = case_when(
      supported & str_starts(hypothesis, "H3") ~
        paste0("Supported (higher odds in ", higher_sex, ")"),
      supported  ~ "Supported",
      .default   = "Not supported at alpha = 0.10"
    ))

  results
}

# Sentence fragment describing one adjusted odds ratio, e.g.
# "1.85 times the odds (90% CI 1.75 to 1.96)"
describe_or <- function(or_table, term_name) {
  row <- filter(or_table, term == term_name)
  direction <- if (row$adj_or >= 1) "higher" else "lower"
  pct <- abs(row$adj_or - 1)
  paste0(sprintf("%.2f", row$adj_or), " times the odds (", ci_label, " ",
         sprintf("%.2f", row$adj_low), " to ", sprintf("%.2f", row$adj_high),
         "), i.e., ", fmt_pct(pct, 0), " ", direction, " odds")
}

# Race/ethnicity groups whose adjusted OR CI lies entirely above / below 1
race_groups_by_direction <- function(or_table) {
  race_rows <- filter(or_table, str_starts(term, "race_eth")) |>
    mutate(group = str_remove(term, "^race_eth"))
  list(
    higher = race_rows |> filter(adj_low > 1) |> pull(group),
    lower  = race_rows |> filter(adj_high < 1) |> pull(group),
    unclear = race_rows |> filter(adj_low <= 1, adj_high >= 1) |> pull(group)
  )
}

# Why hypertension matters

High blood pressure (hypertension) is one of the most common chronic conditions in the United States and one of the most important. It usually causes no symptoms, yet it is a major risk factor for heart disease and stroke, the leading causes of death in the country, as well as for kidney disease. Because it is silent, many people do not know they have it until damage has already occurred.

It is also common. Using the 2017 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) definition, the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) estimated that 45.4% of U.S. adults had hypertension in 2017–2018 [1] and 47.7% in August 2021–August 2023 [2]. Both reports found that prevalence rises sharply with age and is higher in men than in women. That combination of a high burden, a long symptom-free period, and well-established treatments makes it worth understanding *who* is most likely to have high blood pressure.

For this project I asked a focused version of that question:

> **What demographic and anthropometric factors are associated with hypertension among U.S. adults?**

# Why NHANES is a good fit

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) is run by the CDC's National Center for Health Statistics. It is well suited to this question for three reasons:

1. **Blood pressure and body size are measured, not self-reported.** Each participant visits a Mobile Examination Center, where trained staff take up to three oscillometric blood pressure readings and measure height and weight. Self-reported weight tends to be underestimated, and many people with high blood pressure do not know it, so measured values are a real advantage.
2. **It links examination and interview data** for the same person through a shared identifier (SEQN). That let me combine measured blood pressure with a questionnaire item about blood-pressure medication, which is needed to identify people whose hypertension is controlled by treatment.
3. **It is large and diverse.** NHANES deliberately over-samples several groups, including Hispanic, Non-Hispanic Black, and Non-Hispanic Asian adults, so there are enough people in every race/ethnicity group to compare them.

I used the **2017–March 2020 pre-pandemic** release, as required for Project A. It combines the full 2017–2018 cycle with the part of the 2019–2020 cycle completed before fieldwork stopped in March 2020 because of COVID-19.

# Research question and hypotheses

Before looking at any results, I wrote down four hypotheses based on the NCHS reports and on basic physiology:

| | Hypothesis | Main reasoning |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **H1** | Older adults have a higher prevalence of hypertension. | Arteries stiffen with age, raising systolic pressure. |
| **H2** | Individuals with higher BMI have a higher prevalence of hypertension. | Excess body weight is linked to higher blood volume, sympathetic activity, and insulin resistance. |
| **H3** | Hypertension prevalence differs by biological sex. | National reports show higher prevalence in men, especially at younger ages. |
| **H4** | Hypertension prevalence differs across race/ethnicity groups. | National reports show the highest prevalence among Non-Hispanic Black adults. |

- **P_DEMO (Demographics):** interview/examination status, age, sex, and race/ethnicity.

- **P_BPXO (Blood Pressure – Oscillometric):** up to three systolic and three diastolic readings.
- **P_BPQ (Blood Pressure & Cholesterol questionnaire):** whether the participant currently takes prescribed medicine for high blood pressure.

This meets the Project A requirement of using the Demographics file plus at least two files from the Examination, Laboratory, or Questionnaire groups (here, two Examination files and one Questionnaire file). The codebook below lists every variable in the final analytic data set.

## How I defined hypertension

My primary outcome follows the definition used in both NCHS reports [1, 2], which comes from the 2017 ACC/AHA guideline [3]: a participant has hypertension if their **average systolic pressure is at least 130 mmHg, or their average diastolic pressure is at least 80 mmHg, or they report currently taking prescribed medicine for high blood pressure**. Including medication matters: a person whose blood pressure is well controlled by treatment still has hypertension.

I was careful not to over-read what this variable measures. NHANES measures blood pressure at a single visit, while a clinical diagnosis requires elevated readings on at least two separate occasions. My outcome is therefore a *survey classification* of hypertension, which is what national surveillance reports use, rather than a clinical diagnosis. Because the 130/80 threshold is newer and lower than the older 140/90 threshold from the JNC 7 report, I also repeated the main analyses with the 140/90 definition to see whether any conclusion depended on that choice.

# Data preprocessing

All downloading, merging, and cleaning happens in one R script (`R/prepare_nhanes_data.R`, shown in full in the [appendix](#appendix-full-r-code)), so the process can be rerun from the raw public files. The steps were:

1. **Download** the four tables with the `nhanesA` package, keeping the original numeric codes (`translated = FALSE`) so that every recode is explicit, and **cache** each raw table as an `.Rds` file so later renders do not download it again.
2. **Merge** the tables with `left_join()` on SEQN, starting from Demographics, and standardize names with `janitor::clean_names()`.
3. **Restrict** to participants who were both interviewed and examined (`RIDSTATR = 2`) and aged 21 to 79. NHANES records everyone aged 80 and over as "80," so their true age is unknown; the course guidance also recommends analyzing adults only.
4. **Average blood pressure** over the available oscillometric readings, treating any diastolic reading of 0 mmHg as missing.
5. **Code medication use** while respecting the questionnaire's skip pattern. Only people who had been told they had high blood pressure (BPQ020) *and* had been told to take medicine for it (BPQ040A) were asked whether they currently take it (BPQ050A). Anyone who answered "no" to an earlier question was coded as not taking medication, and "Refused" or "Don't know" answers were set to missing.
6. **Label factors** with meaningful levels and sensible reference groups (Female for sex; Non-Hispanic White, the largest group, for race/ethnicity).
7. **Keep complete cases** on every analytic variable, then run automatic checks (`stopifnot()`) that the sample size falls within the course range of 500 to 7,500, that identifiers are unique, that ages, BMI, and blood pressure are within plausible ranges, and that every race/ethnicity group has at least 100 participants.
8. **Create display categories** for age and BMI. Age and BMI stay **continuous** in the regression model; the categories are used only to show prevalence in tables and bar charts.

# Statistical methods

Following the course convention, I report **`r ci_label`s** and use a significance level of **α = 0.10** throughout. I chose each method to match the type of comparison:

- **Prevalence within groups:** proportion with hypertension, with Wilson score confidence intervals, which behave well even for smaller groups.
- **Association between a grouping variable and hypertension:** Pearson chi-square test of independence. For the two ordered variables (age group and BMI category), I added the **Cochran–Armitage test for trend**, which asks the more specific question of whether prevalence rises (or falls) steadily across ordered groups.
- **Sex (a 2 × 2 table):** difference in prevalence between men and women with a `r ci_label`.
- **BMI by hypertension status:** Welch two-sample *t*-test for the difference in mean BMI. BMI is right-skewed, but with thousands of participants per group the sampling distribution of the mean is close to normal; I also report medians as a check.
- **Multivariable model:** logistic regression of hypertension on age (per 10 years), BMI (per 5 kg/m²), sex, and race/ethnicity. I present adjusted odds ratios with profile-likelihood `r ci_label`s next to unadjusted odds ratios, a likelihood-ratio test for each variable (which gives one overall test for the six-level race/ethnicity variable), the C-statistic for discrimination, and a decile calibration plot.
- **Sensitivity analysis:** the same model refit with the 140/90 mmHg definition.

Rescaling age to 10-year units and BMI to 5-unit steps does not change the model; it only makes each odds ratio describe a difference that is meaningful in practice.

# Interpretation

Taken together, the results describe a clear pattern in this sample. The age gradient was large (from `r fmt_pct(age_young$prevalence, 0)` to `r fmt_pct(age_old$prevalence, 0)` across the three age groups), which fits the well-known stiffening of large arteries with age and the NCHS finding that more than 70% of adults aged 60 and over have hypertension [2]. The BMI odds ratio was estimated *after* accounting for age, sex, and race/ethnicity, so the BMI pattern `r if (filter(or_tbl, term == 'bmi_5')$significant) 'is not simply a by-product of differences in age or the other variables' else 'was weaker than the unadjusted comparisons suggested once the other variables were included'`. `r if (filter(or_tbl, term == 'sexMale')$adj_or > 1) 'Men had higher adjusted odds than women, the same direction as in the national reports [1, 2].' else 'Unlike the national reports, where prevalence is higher in men [1, 2], men in this sample did not have higher adjusted odds than women.'`

The race/ethnicity results need the most careful reading. Race/ethnicity is a self-reported social category, not a biological measurement, so differences between groups are best understood as markers of differences in social and economic conditions, access to care, stress, diet, and other exposures that this analysis did not measure. The adjusted model tells us whether a gap remains after accounting for age, BMI, and sex; it does not tell us *why* it exists.

Finally, it is important to be precise about what "hypertension" means here. It is a classification based on blood pressure measured at one visit plus self-reported medication, which is how national surveillance defines it, but it is not the same as a clinical diagnosis.

# Limitations

- **Unweighted analysis.** I did not use the NHANES sampling weights, strata, or clusters, as specified for Project A. Because NHANES over-samples some groups, my percentages describe this sample and are not estimates of national prevalence.
- **Cross-sectional data.** The survey measures everything at one time, so it can show association but not cause and effect, or which came first.
- **Single-visit blood pressure.** Readings from one visit can be affected by temporary factors such as the "white coat" effect, which may lead to some misclassification.
- **Self-reported medication.** Medication use depends on accurate recall and on the questionnaire's skip pattern.
- **Omitted variables.** Income, education, smoking, alcohol, physical activity, diet, diabetes, and kidney function were not included and may confound the associations shown.
- **Model assumptions.** The model assumes that age and BMI have a straight-line relationship with the log-odds of hypertension, and BMI is only an indirect measure of body fat. Odds ratios also overstate risk ratios when an outcome is this common.
- **Complete-case analysis.** Excluding participants with missing data could bias the results if missingness was related to blood pressure.
- **Data age.** The data were collected before March 2020; patterns may have changed since then.

# Conclusions

In `r fmt_int(kpi$n)` NHANES adults aged 21–79, hypertension was common (`r fmt_pct(kpi$prevalence)` of the sample under the 130/80 definition). After adjustment for the other factors, each additional 10 years of age carried `r sprintf('%.2f', filter(or_tbl, term == 'age_10')$adj_or)` times the odds of hypertension, each additional 5 kg/m² of BMI carried `r sprintf('%.2f', filter(or_tbl, term == 'bmi_5')$adj_or)` times the odds, and men had `r sprintf('%.2f', filter(or_tbl, term == 'sexMale')$adj_or)` times the odds of women; the overall test for race/ethnicity gave p `r fmt_p_inline(race_lrt_p)`. The hypothesis scorecard in @tbl-hypotheses summarizes the formal decision for each hypothesis.

Because PPDAC is a cycle, these conclusions point to the next problem to study. A natural second cycle would (1) use the survey weights to produce national estimates, (2) add socioeconomic and lifestyle variables to understand *why* the race/ethnicity and sex differences exist, (3) allow non-linear age and BMI effects, (4) extend the analysis to the August 2021–August 2023 NHANES cycle, and (5) study hypertension awareness and control, which are more directly actionable for public health than prevalence alone.
