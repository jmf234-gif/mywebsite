What factors underlie High Blood Pressure? A hypothesis about age, BMI, sex, and race/ethnicity in NHANES 2017–2020

A blog post for PQHS 431 Project A Task B

A blog accompanying my hypertension dashboard: the data, the four hypotheses, how the dashboard was built, and what the results show.

Dataset and Research Question

I used the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2017–March 2020 pre-pandemic data. NHANES combines a home interview with a physical exam, so blood pressure, height, and weight are measured by trained staff instead of self-reported. I merged four NHANES data files (demographics, body measures, blood pressure readings, and a blood pressure questionnaire), keeping adults aged 21 to 79 that had complete data. This left 7,019 adults.

My question was: What demographic and anthropometric factors are associated with hypertension among U.S. adults?

A participant was counted as having hypertension if the average of their blood pressure readings was at least 130/80 mmHg, or if they reported currently taking blood pressure medicine. This is a survey classification based on one exam visit, not a clinical diagnosis, which would need elevated readings at more than one visit.

Hypotheses

  • H1: If age is associated with hypertension, then older adults will have a higher prevalence of hypertension than younger adults.
  • H2: If BMI is associated with hypertension, then hypertension prevalence will increase as BMI increases.
  • H3: If biological sex is associated with hypertension, then hypertension prevalence will differ between men and women.
  • H4: If race/ethnicity is associated with hypertension, then hypertension prevalence will show clear differences across backgrounds.

Building the Dashboard

I built the dashboard in R with Quarto’s dashboard format and added it to my existing Quarto website. Thus, it shares the site’s menu and colors. The data are downloaded with the nhanesA package and cleaned in one R script, and a second script runs the analyses. The dashboard and this post both use those scripts, so the numbers always match.

The dashboard pages follow the order of the analysis:

  • Overview: Introduces the study population (sample size, mean age, and mean BMI) and hypertension prevalence across the sample.
  • Demographics: Characteristics of the adults included in the analysis.
  • BMI & Hypertension and Demographic Factors: Visual comparisons used to evaluate the four hypotheses.
  • Model: Results from the adjusted multivariable model.
  • Explore: Interactive exploration of hypertension across participant groups.
  • Conclusions and Methods Key findings and a summary of the analytical approach.

Each plot has a brief explanation next to it, and the interactive charts show exact numbers when you hover over them.

Analyses Performed

For each factor, I compared the percentage of adults with hypertension across groups (age groups, BMI categories, men and women, and six race/ethnicity groups), and tested whether those percentages differed. I then fit a logistic regression model that includes age, BMI, sex, and race/ethnicity together. This analysis shows whether each factor is still linked to hypertension when comparing people who are similar on the other three. As taught in our course lectures, I used 90% confidence intervals.

Key Results

Overall, 54.6% of the 7,019 adults in the sample met the hypertension definition.

  • Age (H1): 26.4% of adults aged 21–39 had hypertension, compared with 77.9% of adults aged 60–79. In the model, each additional 10 years of age was associated with 1.94 times the odds of hypertension.
  • BMI (H2): 39.8% of adults at a healthy weight (BMI 18.5–24.9) had hypertension, compared with 67.9% of adults with a BMI of 35 or higher. Each additional 5 kg/m² of BMI was associated with 1.39 times the odds.
  • Sex (H3): 58.3% of men and 51.0% of women had hypertension. Men had 1.51 times the odds of women with the same age, BMI, and race/ethnicity.
  • Race/ethnicity (H4): Prevalence ranged from 43.8% among Mexican American adults to 65.5% among Non-Hispanic Black adults. After accounting for age, BMI, and sex, the odds were higher than for Non-Hispanic White adults among Non-Hispanic Black, Non-Hispanic Asian and Other/Multiracial adults and lower among Mexican American adults.

All four hypotheses were supported by these data.

Conclusions

In this sample, hypertension was broadly common amongst participants. Hypertension was determined to be more common in older adults, in adults with higher BMI, and in men. It also differed across the race/ethnicity groups. Age was the factor that showed the largest difference between groups.

These results have limits:

  • They describe this sample only. NHANES deliberately over-samples some groups, and I did not use the survey weights. Thus, the percentages cannot act as estimates for all U.S. adults.
  • They show association, not cause. The data come from only one point in time, so they show which characteristics go together, not what causes hypertension.
  • Race/ethnicity is self-reported. Differences between groups may reflect factors this analysis did not measure, such as income or access to care.

The dashboard’s Explore page lets you filter by age, sex, and race/ethnicity to see how prevalence changes across BMI categories in different subgroups.
