Study objective

Research question. What demographic and anthropometric factors are associated with hypertension among U.S. adults?

This dashboard investigates hypertension in 7,019 adults aged 21 to 79, who participated in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). My dashboard tests whether four pre-specified hypotheses are associated with hypertension including age, body mass index (BMI), biological sex, and race/ethnicity. We will first assess one factor at a time and then altogether in a logistic regression model.

Key numbers. In this sample, 54.6% of adults (90% CI 53.6% to 55.6%) met the hypertension definition below. The mean age was 49.4 years (SD 16.0), and the mean BMI was 30.2 kg/m² (SD 7.6).

How hypertension is defined here. A participant is classified as having hypertension if their average oscillometric blood pressure reading was at least 130 mmHg systolic or at least 80 mmHg diastolic, or if they reported currently taking prescribed medicine for high blood pressure. This is the 2017 American Heart Association (AHA) threshold used in recent reports. Because every result is re-estimated with the older 140/90 mmHg threshold as a sensitivity check (39.6% prevalence under that rule), conclusions can be checked against the choice of cut-point.

Hypotheses
Hypothesis Primary evidence
H1 Older adults have a higher prevalence of hypertension. Prevalence by age group; age odds ratio
H2 Individuals with higher BMI have a higher prevalence of hypertension. Prevalence by BMI category; BMI odds ratio
H3 Hypertension prevalence differs by biological sex. 2 × 2 comparison; sex odds ratio
H4 Hypertension prevalence differs across race/ethnicity groups. 6 × 2 chi-square; likelihood-ratio test

All intervals are 90% CIs and all tests use α = 0.10, following the course convention.

About the data: NHANES 2017–March 2020

NHANES is a continuous survey run by the CDC’s National Center for Health Statistics. It combines a household interview with a standardized physical examination in a Mobile Examination Center, so blood pressure, height, and weight are measured by trained staff rather than self-reported. The 2017–March 2020 pre-pandemic file combines the 2017–2018 cycle with the partial 2019–2020 cycle that ended when fieldwork was suspended because of COVID-19.

Four public-use files were merged by the respondent identifier (SEQN): Demographics (P_DEMO), Body Measures (P_BMX), Blood Pressure – Oscillometric (P_BPXO), and the Blood Pressure & Cholesterol questionnaire (P_BPQ).

What these numbers do — and do not — mean
  • Results describe this unweighted sample of NHANES participants. Notably, NHANES deliberately over-samples some groups, so these percentages are not survey-weighted national prevalence estimates.
  • Blood pressure was measured at one examination visit. A clinical diagnosis of hypertension requires elevated readings on two or more occasions, so “hypertension” here is a survey-based classification, not a diagnosis.
  • The data are cross-sectional: showing which characteristics go together, not what causes hypertension.

Who is in the sample?

The analytic sample contains 7,019 adults aged 21 to 79 with complete data on every study variable. Ages are spread across the full range (mean 49.4, SD 16.0 years). The colors in the histogram show how the share of adults with hypertension changes across ages, previewing H1.

Women make up 50.8% of the sample and men 49.2%. Race/ethnicity is self-reported using the six NHANES categories. Non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic, and Non-Hispanic Asian adults are over-sampled by design in NHANES, which is why their respective shares in the data herein are larger than in the U.S. population and why every group has enough participants for stable estimates (smallest group: 347).

Interpretation

Adults classified as having hypertension had a mean BMI 2.9 kg/m² higher than those without hypertension (90% CI 2.6 to 3.2). Prevalence rose across BMI categories, from 39.8% at a healthy weight to 67.9% among adults with class 2–3 obesity, and the test for trend was statistically significant (p < 0.001). The BMI distribution is right-skewed, so the Welch t-test relies on the large sample (central limit theorem); the medians tell the same story (30.3 vs. 27.4 kg/m²).

These are unadjusted comparisons. Because BMI, age, sex, and race/ethnicity are related to one another, the Model page asks whether BMI remains associated with hypertension after accounting for the other three factors.

Interpretation

Age (H1). Prevalence rose with age, from 26.4% among adults 21–39 to 77.9% among adults 60–79 (Cochran–Armitage trend test p < 0.001).

Sex (H3). Prevalence was 58.3% in men and 51.0% in women, a difference of 7.2 percentage points (men minus women; 90% CI 5.3 to 9.2; p < 0.001).

Race/ethnicity (H4). Unadjusted prevalence was highest among Non-Hispanic Black adults (65.5%) and lowest among Mexican American adults (43.8%); the chi-square test of independence gave p < 0.001.

Error bars are 90% CIs (Wilson method). Because the race/ethnicity groups differ in age and BMI, these unadjusted gaps are revisited in the adjusted model. Race/ethnicity is a social category; differences between groups are best read as markers of differing social, economic, and health-care exposures rather than as biological differences.

Model summary

Holding the other variables constant, each additional 10 years of age was associated with 1.94 times the odds (90% CI 1.88 to 2.01), i.e., 94% higher odds, and each additional 5 kg/m² of BMI with 1.39 times the odds (90% CI 1.34 to 1.44), i.e., 39% higher odds. Men had 1.51 times the odds (90% CI 1.38 to 1.65), i.e., 51% higher odds compared with women of the same age, BMI, and race/ethnicity.

For race/ethnicity (likelihood-ratio test p < 0.001), groups with adjusted odds clearly above Non-Hispanic White adults were: Non-Hispanic Black, Non-Hispanic Asian and Other/Multiracial; clearly below: Mexican American; interval including 1: Other Hispanic.

Model quality. The C-statistic of 0.789 means that, for a randomly chosen person with and without hypertension, the model assigns the higher predicted risk to the person with hypertension 79% of the time. Across the ten deciles of predicted risk, observed and predicted prevalence differ by at most 3.0 percentage points (see Calibration). Some estimates change direction or significance under the stricter 140/90 mmHg threshold: Mexican American (vs. Non-Hispanic White) and Other Hispanic (vs. Non-Hispanic White). Those particular results depend on the definition used.

Caution. Odds ratios are not risk ratios; with a common outcome like this one they overstate the relative difference in prevalence. The model assumes a straight-line relationship between age or BMI and the log-odds of hypertension.

Summary of findings
  • Age (H1): prevalence rose from 26.4% in adults 21–39 to 77.9% in adults 60–79, and each decade of age carried 1.94 times the odds after adjustment.
  • BMI (H2): the adjusted odds ratio was 1.39 per 5 kg/m² (90% CI 1.34 to 1.44), after accounting for age, sex, and race/ethnicity.
  • Sex (H3): men had 1.51 times the adjusted odds of women.
  • Race/ethnicity (H4): after adjustment, Non-Hispanic Black, Non-Hispanic Asian and Other/Multiracial had higher odds than Non-Hispanic White adults, and Mexican American had lower odds.
  • Robustness: Some estimates change direction or significance under the stricter 140/90 mmHg threshold: Mexican American (vs. Non-Hispanic White) and Other Hispanic (vs. Non-Hispanic White). Those particular results depend on the definition used.
Public health interpretation

Roughly 55% of adults in this sample met the current (130/80 mmHg) hypertension threshold, so elevated blood pressure is common, not exceptional. Age cannot be changed, but the steep age gradient supports regular blood-pressure screening throughout adulthood, not only in older age. BMI is the one potentially modifiable factor studied here. An association that persists after adjustment is consistent with guidance that weight management is part of blood-pressure prevention, although this cross-sectional analysis cannot show that lowering BMI lowers blood pressure. Differences by race/ethnicity that remain after adjustment point to the value of equitable access to screening and treatment, and to studying the social and structural factors behind them.

Limitations
  • Not nationally representative as analyzed. Survey weights, strata, and clusters were not used (course design), so percentages describe this sample only.
  • Single-visit measurement. Blood pressure was averaged over up to three readings at one visit; clinical diagnosis requires repeated visits.
  • Self-reported medication may be misclassified, and people with controlled hypertension who stopped medication are missed.
  • Cross-sectional design shows association, not causation or temporal order.
  • Residual confounding. Income, education, diet, physical activity, alcohol, smoking, diabetes, and kidney disease were not included.
  • Model form. Age and BMI are assumed to act linearly on the log-odds scale, and BMI is an imperfect measure of body fat.
  • Complete-case analysis may introduce bias if missing data were related to blood pressure.
Future directions: the next PPDAC cycle
  • Problem: Do these associations hold for the whole U.S. population, and are they explained by social and lifestyle factors?
  • Plan: Use the NHANES survey design (weights, strata, clusters) and add income, education, smoking, and physical activity.
  • Data: Extend to the August 2021–August 2023 NHANES cycle to check whether patterns changed after the pandemic.
  • Analysis: Allow non-linear age and BMI effects (restricted cubic splines) and test age × sex interaction.
  • Conclusion: Move beyond prevalence to hypertension awareness and control, where public health action is most direct.

Data currency and reproducibility
  • Data timestamps: NHANES files downloaded 2026-09-27; dashboard last rendered 2026-09-28 18:10 EDT.
  • Data source: NHANES 2017-March 2020 pre-pandemic public-use files (P_DEMO, P_BMX, P_BPXO, P_BPQ), obtained with the nhanesA R package (version 1.4.1).
  • Methods: Wilson 90% CIs for proportions; Pearson chi-square and Cochran–Armitage trend tests; Welch t-test; multivariable logistic regression with profile-likelihood 90% CIs and likelihood-ratio tests; α = 0.10 throughout.
  • Software: R version 4.6.1 (2026-06-24); Analyses were conducted in R and presented through a Quarto dashboard.
  • Accessibility: Color-blind-friendly visualizations, written interpretations for figures, and a mobile-friendly layout were used throughout.
